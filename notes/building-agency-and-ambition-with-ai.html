Building agency and ambition

Recently, I’ve been thinking about what it means to push the scope of our ambitions. The bar has never been lower, but also higher at the same time. What does that mean exactly? Miles Cranmer’s lecture at Cambridge University on AI brain rot illustrates this phenomenon with one particular visual that stuck with me. Pre-AI, there was this wide bell curve, with the majority of people sitting somewhere in the middle as average, and fewer scattered across the weaker (left tail) and above-average (right tail) ends. The curve was wide because of the genuinely large variance in people’s hard skills and effort. But with AI, we may be starting to see this curve compress, as individuals across the spectrum gain access to capabilities that were previously unevenly distributed. A 2023 BCG study offers an interesting illustration of this. In an experiment involving 750 consultants completing 18 tasks, bottom-half performers improved their performance by 43% with GPT-4 assistance, compared with 17% for top-half performers.

Of course, this doesn’t mean AI is making everyone equally capable. But it does suggest that some of the differences we previously attributed to hard skills and effort may become less visible in the quality of work people can produce.

Beyond that, I hypothesize that the more underlying shift isn’t necessarily a skill issue, but an ambition issue. Cranmer argues in his lecture that we must resist the tendency toward passive AI use, which for many people currently looks like (1) information gathering and learning, or (2) task assistance. The challenge is not just to use AI more for the sake of it, but to move beyond the things we’re already doing and towards what else we could be doing in the first place. This requires being more ambitious with how we use AI, and unafraid to chart new territories to discover just how much bigger the world of possibilities could be.

When I think about this, I imagine myself playing the role of an explorer, venturing into the wilderness with a compass, an empty map, and a pen in hand, discovering new possibilities for what we can achieve. It’s both frightening and exhilarating, but I believe it’s a special and increasingly important role to play as society advances. We should encourage one another to see beyond our existing field of view and expand what we believe is possible.

Sahil Bloom wrote a thread about the relationships that change our lives. If we’re lucky, there are or will be people in our lives willing to bend their knees and let us stand on their shoulders so we can see a view higher than we could have ever imagined on our own. He calls this the high shoulder theory. Not only do these people broaden our perspective of the world and what’s possible, but the ones who truly change our lives are those who are also willing to walk alongside us. I believe we all deserve to have these kinds of relationships, and should also strive to be a shoulder for others.

The reason I relate to this theory isn’t just personal. It’s also forced me to think about how I can continuously broaden my own perspective of what’s possible so that I can become a higher shoulder for others to stand on.

As much as I don’t want to settle for being average myself, I also don’t want the people around me to settle for it either. Personally, my measure of success isn’t based on how much money I can make or how much more I can have. Success, to me, is about how many people I can help and lift up along the way. And certainly, I want my future kids to have the privilege of growing up with a sense that the world is full of opportunities. So, how can we push ourselves to become more ambitious and expand our scope of what’s possible?

Before answering that, I feel like I have to address the root of the question: what does it actually mean to be ambitious? One person’s ambition can look very different from another’s. Ambition is subjective, and I’m generally against prescribing what people should aspire to. For instance, one person might be extremely ambitious about building a billion-dollar business and changing millions of lives. Another person’s ambition might be to take really good care of their family and friends, making a meaningful impact on an individual level. Someone else might be ambitious about building a life that gives them the time and freedom to pursue their passions and hobbies.

Our scopes of ambition are all different, and I think they ultimately come down to one simple question: What more do you want to do with your life? Do you want to increase the number of people you impact? Make more time for your kids and relationships? Pursue more of your interests and enjoy life? Whatever that looks like, I believe we should continuously expand our sense of what’s possible and pursue more of what we want out of life.

In Cranmer’s presentation, he illustrates that to push to the right side of the bell curve, you have to be willing to ride the wave and not go against it. But what does that look like in practice? How do we move beyond using AI for productivity gains and start expanding the scope of what we can accomplish? Here’s my current draft mental model. The general idea is that there are two dimensions to AI leverage: execution leverage, or the amount of work we can accomplish per unit of human effort, and ambition leverage, or the extent to which AI expands the set of outcomes we can realistically pursue. The first three stages focus on increasing the scope and reliability of delegation. The last two focus on translating those capabilities into increasingly ambitious outcomes.

(1) Start with basic task execution. Start with individual tasks that have clearly defined inputs, instructions, constraints, and expected outputs. The objective is to establish a baseline understanding of what AI can and cannot reliably execute. Ethan Mollick describes AI’s capabilities as a jagged frontier, where AI can perform remarkably well on certain complex tasks while failing at seemingly simpler ones. This means capability shouldn’t be assumed based on task complexity alone. It needs to be tested. For a given task, evaluate output quality, consistency across attempts, failure modes, and the amount of human correction required. You’re effectively mapping which types of work can be delegated reliably under which conditions.

(2) Delegate a process. Once individual tasks become reliable, start combining them into repeatable workflows with defined inputs, outputs, intermediate steps, and validation checks. The unit of delegation shifts from a single task to a process. Rather than manually initiating and supervising each step, you’re designing a system that can execute a sequence of activities with minimal intervention. The important metric here isn’t the number of tasks automated, but the reduction in total human effort. A workflow that previously required five hours of manual work but now requires two hours of prompting, reviewing, and correcting has created three hours of time savings. A workflow that runs autonomously but consistently produces errors requiring extensive rework may create little or no leverage. The objective is to improve the ratio of useful, validated output to human time invested.

(3) Expand the scope of ownership. At this stage, the unit of delegation shifts again, from predefined workflows to projects or outcomes. Instead of specifying how every step should be completed, define the objective, available resources, constraints, quality standards, and success criteria. AI can then decompose the problem, plan the work, execute multiple steps, use tools, and iterate based on feedback. In the allocation economy, we want to point toward a shift in the human role, from primarily executing work to directing and allocating resources. But autonomy isn’t binary. A useful way to evaluate it is by measuring the scope of work AI can complete before requiring human intervention, alongside the quality and reliability of the resulting outcome. The objective isn’t necessarily to eliminate human involvement. It’s to move human attention toward higher-leverage activities such as defining objectives, evaluating trade-offs, handling exceptions, and making consequential decisions.

(4) Expand your concept of capability. The first three stages are primarily concerned with improving the efficiency and reliability of execution, which, well, just takes you to the new average baseline. We could become extremely efficient at doing the same work we’ve always done without questioning whether we should be doing something different. I think of this stage as shifting our feasibility frontier: the set of outcomes we can realistically pursue given our constraints. Every objective has an associated cost of execution, whether measured in time, money, expertise, coordination, or risk. Historically, these constraints have determined which problems we consider worth attempting. AI can change that calculation. We’re no longer optimizing the execution of an existing process. We’re redesigning the system around a different and potentially more valuable outcome.

(5) Keep pushing the boundaries. Once we begin questioning which constraints still apply, the next step is to deliberately test the boundaries of what’s possible. I think of this as a process of hypothesis-driven experimentation. Start by identifying an objective that currently appears beyond your capabilities. Break it down into the constraints preventing you from pursuing it, whether those are technical, financial, operational, or knowledge-related. Then identify which constraints AI might reduce and design an experiment to test that hypothesis. Rather than attempting to build the entire solution immediately, test the riskiest assumption first.

For example, if you want to build an AI system that predicts business performance, the first experiment might not be building the full system. It might be testing whether an AI-assisted forecasting workflow can produce useful predictions against historical data, and whether those predictions outperform a simple baseline. If it works, you’ve established a new capability. If it fails, you’ve learned which constraint remains unresolved. Either outcome provides information that can inform the next experiment. The process becomes a feedback loop: identify a constraint, form a hypothesis, run an experiment, measure the result, update your model of what’s feasible, and attempt something more ambitious. Over time, these experiments can progressively expand your understanding of the possibility space.

Every experiment requires us to make an assumption about what’s possible, take action, observe the outcome, and update our understanding. Sometimes our assumptions are validated. Sometimes they fail miserably. But either way, we’re learning to distinguish between constraints that are genuinely fixed and those that we’ve simply never tested.

My hypothesis is that repeatedly engaging in this process changes how we approach problems more generally. Instead of immediately ruling out an idea because we lack the time, expertise, or resources, we become more inclined to investigate which constraints actually prevent us from pursuing it, and whether those constraints can be overcome.

Perhaps the most meaningful opportunity with AI isn’t simply that it allows us to accomplish more work. It’s that it gives us a practical mechanism for continuously revising our assumptions about what we’re capable of attempting in the first place.
