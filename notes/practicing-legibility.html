Practicing legibility

If you’ve ever been told, more than once, that you’re “too hard to read”, this is a bug, not a feature. You have a legibility problem.

While there are instances where keeping cards close to your chest is the strategic move against opponents, I doubt a majority of us are required to live like professional poker players or a special ops agent. My guess is that more often than not, we would benefit much more from making allies than enemies. To form connections or partnerships, applicable to both your personal and professional life, requires the following:

(1) Shared honesty: The ability to first be clear and honest with yourself. What do I actually think? What do I want? What am I feeling? What matters to me? What am I uncertain about? You can’t make yourself legible to someone else if you’re illegible to yourself.

(2) Shared vulnerability: The willingness to make some of that internal reality visible to another person. To translate what you think, feel, want, or see into its simplest coherent form—and then actually express it.

(3) Shared experience: Once you’ve made yourself legible, you have to let the other person participate. Give them enough information to respond, offer feedback, express their own reality, and make their own decisions. You aren’t trying to control their response; you’re giving them something real to respond to.

Something beautiful happens when you find people that want to participate with you equally—you form a genuine connection. It’s all easier said than done though because at every step, there is a risk of failure mode.

Risks of legibility

Being honest risks confronting uncomfortable truths.

Ambiguity is a nuisance, but also a protective shield. As long as you remain ambiguous about something, you can preserve as many options as possible and never confront making deliberate choices—choices not made for you, but by you. This can be uncomfortable because (1) it makes you accountable for the decision made, and (2) you might discover that the choices in front of you may be inconvenient, unrealistic, contradictory, or different from what you thought you should want. You have to be honest with yourself.

Being vulnerable risks being rejected.

The gap between internal and external expression is probably where heaviest failure happens. Although we may have confronted our internal realities, many of us get stuck because we are either (1) unconsciously conforming ourselves to the current existing reality, or (2) communicating poorly to make it clear for someone else to understand us. This can look like not saying what we actually mean or overcomplicating the core message we want to get across. Both modes of failure protect us from rejection in different ways. Remaining illegible protects you from receiving a definitive answer or lets you dodge around direct disappointment.

Sharing an experience risks loss of control. Your honest invitation toward someone does not guarantee that they will accept it. Once you put yourself out there and make the bid, you lose control over what happens next. The other person comes with their own thoughts, desires, constraints, interpretations, and agency. They can accept, reject, misunderstand, counter, or make a completely different move. You have to loosen your grip enough to let another person participate freely.

Practicing legibility

On a more optimistic note, legibility is largely a skill issue, and skills can be practiced. Here are some of the strategies I’ve been trying:

Make the internal coherent. I’ve started brain dumping more. I’ll use voice notes or dictation to get the raw thoughts out, then feed them to ChatGPT to return a simplified, coherent version of what I’m trying to say. Sometimes I don’t actually know what I think until I hear myself trying to explain it.

Say less. It’s easy to overexplain, overcomplicate, or add a bunch of jargon that buries the actual message. Compression and brevity are skills. I’ve been practicing leading with the point by asking myself: If this person remembers one thing from what I’m about to say, what should it be? Say that first. Additional context or explanation can come afterwards or in the additional readings / footnotes.

Close the loop. I’ve started trying to make communication end somewhere concrete: What did we decide? What happens next? Who owns it? Did what I meant actually land? Expression without feedback still leaves room for ambiguity.

Illegibility is a burden to others

Being hard to read or understand means there is a gap between your internal reality and what you’ve made available externally. If you don’t do the work of closing that gap, you’re simply handing that work to someone else.

People are then forced to interpret signals, read between the lines, ask follow-up questions, chase you for information, or guess what you actually mean. At work, someone has to figure out whether you’re aligned, blocked, unhappy, or waiting for something. In relationships, someone has to figure out whether you’re interested, upset, distant, or simply processing. I can guarantee you that no one wants to take on more cognitive work than necessary.

So the kind and generous thing to do is to make yourself as legible as possible in service of others, but also yourself. Because taking the responsibility to make yourself legible allows others to participate in building something with you.
